LaTeX Reference

Published

August 24, 2026

Overview

LaTeX is a software system for typesetting documents. It’s based on the TeX typesetting system that was designed and written by computer scientist and Stanford University professor Donald Knuth. LaTeX provides a high-level, descriptive markup language to use TeX more easily: TeX handles the document layout, while LaTeX handles the content side for document processing. Because the plain TeX formatting commands are elementary, it provides authors with ready-made commands for formatting and layout requirements such as chapter headings, footnotes, cross-references and bibliographies.

History

When the first paper volume of Knuth’s The Art of Computer Programming was published in 1968,it was typeset using hot metal typesetting on a Monotype machine. This method, dating back to the 19th century, produced a “classic style” appreciated by Knuth. When the second edition was published, in 1976, the whole book had to be typeset again because the Monotype technology had been largely replaced by phototypesetting, and the original fonts were no longer available. When Knuth received the galley proofs of the new book on March 30 1977, he found them inferior. Disappointed, Knuth set out to design his own typesetting system.

Since the primary goal of the TeX language is high-quality typesetting for publishers of books, Knuth gave a lot of attention to the spacing rules for mathematical formulae. He took three bodies of work that he considered to be standards of excellence for mathematical typography:

  1. Books typeset by the Addison-Wesley Publishing house (the publisher of The Art of Computer Programming).
  2. Editions of the mathematical journal Acta Mathematica from around 1910.
  3. A copy of Indagationes Mathematicae, a Dutch mathematics journal.

Knuth looked closely at these printed papers to sort out a set of rules for spacing. While TeX provides some basic rules and the tools needed to specify proper spacing, the exact parameters depend on the font used to typeset the formula. For example, the spacing for Knuth’s Computer Modern fonts has been precisely fine-tuned over the years and is now set; but when other fonts, such as AMS Euler, were used by Knuth for the first time, new spacing parameters had to be defined.

Guide

Super brief

This is our abridged LaTeX reference. It’s broadly broken down by category.

  • command: each word in the LaTeX vocabulary is a command, though we call them terms in our tables.
  • context: the LaTeX environment in which LaTeX is being executed.
  • environment: they are spaces that have specialized behaviors. We discuss environments briefly just below. And we have a listing of their commands.

Modes

How many modes are there? I don’t know. How many are we concerned with? Two, but we are going to name three of them: inline math, display math, and text mode. This is how the genteel folk refer to them.

Inline Math Mode

Inline math mode is for including mathematical expressions directly within a paragraph of text. It’s used for small formulas, variables, or short equations that don’t need their own line.

  • Delimiters: The most common delimiters are $...$ and \(...\).
  • Examples:
    • The variable $x$ is equal to 3.
    • We see that \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\).

Display Math Mode

Display math mode is for equations that are set on their own separate line. This makes them stand out from the main text and is preferred for longer or more important equations.

  • Delimiters: The most common delimiters are $$...$$ and \[...\].
  • Examples:
    • The Pythagorean theorem is stated as: $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
    • The quadratic formula is: \[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

Environments

Beyond the basic delimiters, you can use specialized environments (like align, equation, gather) for more complex arrangements of equations, such as multi-line equations or systems of equations. These environments automatically switch LaTeX into math mode and handle formatting, spacing, and numbering. For example, \begin{equation} ... \end{equation} is another way to produce a numbered, displayed equation.

Environments

All LaTeX environments follow the same basic syntax pattern:

\begin{environment_name}[optional_parameters]{required_parameters}
  content
\end{environment_name}

Important considerations:

  • Environment names at the beginning and end must match exactly
  • Many environments require specific packages to be loaded (e.g., amsmath for advanced math environments)
  • Some environments can be nested within others
  • Optional parameters are specified in square brackets []
  • Required parameters are specified in curly braces {}
  • The visual results shown are approximations of the actual LaTeX output

Mathematical Environments

Mathematical environments are used for displaying equations, formulas, and mathematical expressions. Some environments stand on their own. Others are dependent on a surrounding environment. For example, align and align* are both standalone environments. They don’t need nobody (Furry sings the blues):

\begin{align}
  a + b &= c + d \\
  e + f &= g + h
\end{align}

On the other hand, we have a chap like aligned. He is called a sub-environment (with love) and must be placed inside another math environment, like equation or gather.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  a + b &= c + d \\
  e + f &= g + h
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
