Propositional Logic

The smallest language that can say true things

Published

November 17, 2026

Overview

Every language in this notebook has three parts: a syntax that says which strings are well formed, a semantics that says what a well-formed string means, and a set of rules for manipulating those strings without changing their meaning. Usually those three parts are buried under decades of practical compromise. C’s grammar runs to dozens of pages, its semantics has to account for undefined behavior, and its “rules of manipulation” are scattered between the standard and the optimizer.

Propositional logic is what you get when you strip all of that away and keep only the skeleton. Its syntax fits on an index card. Its semantics is a single recursive function. Its rules of manipulation are complete, meaning every true statement can be proved, and decidable, meaning a machine can always settle whether a statement is true. It is the smallest language that is still a real language, and it is worth studying on those terms: not as a branch of philosophy, but as the formal system underneath every if, every logic gate, and, as McCulloch and Pitts showed in 1943, the first mathematical model of a neuron.

The thesis of this chapter:

Propositional logic is a language whose only type is the truth value and whose programs are formulas. Everything else (circuits, conditionals, solvers, neurons) is that language running on different hardware.

NoteObject language and metalanguage

A theme that runs through this whole notebook is the difference between a language and the language you use to talk about it. The Quarto chapter has to escape shortcodes because it writes about Quarto in Quarto. Logic makes the distinction explicit. Formulas such as \(p \land q\) belong to the object language. Statements about formulas, such as “\(p \land q\) is satisfiable” or \(\{p, p \to q\} \models q\), belong to the metalanguage. The symbols \(\models\), \(\vdash\), and \(\equiv\) never appear inside a formula; they are commentary on formulas, the way a type checker’s verdict is not part of the program it checks.

A Short History

Propositional logic was discovered twice, about two thousand years apart, and only became a formal language once mathematicians needed one.

The Stoics

Aristotle’s logic (4th century BCE) was a logic of terms: “all men are mortal” relates the categories men and mortal. The Stoics, a generation later, built a logic of whole propositions connected by “and”, “or”, and “if”. Chrysippus (c. 279 to 206 BCE) set out five “indemonstrable” argument forms, including the ones we still call modus ponens and modus tollens. Philo of Megara had already proposed that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false, which is exactly the modern material conditional. The question was hotly contested: the skeptic Sextus Empiricus reports the poet Callimachus joking that even the crows on the rooftops were cawing about which conditionals are true. They still are; see The Conditional.

Leibniz, Boole, and Frege

The Stoic work was largely lost, and logic stayed Aristotelian for most of two millennia. Leibniz dreamed of a calculus ratiocinator in which disputes would be settled by calculation, but never built it. George Boole did, in The Laws of Thought (1854): propositions became algebraic variables, “and” became multiplication, and truth and falsity became 1 and 0. Boole’s algebra had quirks (his “or” was exclusive and he leaned on division), and it took Jevons, Peirce, and others to tidy it into what we now call Boolean algebra.

Gottlob Frege’s Begriffsschrift (1879) is where logic becomes a formal language in the modern sense: a precise syntax, explicit inference rules, and proofs that can be checked mechanically. Frege’s notation was two-dimensional and never caught on, but his program, carried forward by Russell and Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica (1910 to 1913), set the agenda.

Truth Tables, Completeness, and a Single Connective

In 1913 Henry Sheffer showed that a single connective, now called the Sheffer stroke or NAND, can express every other. Charles Peirce had found the same thing around 1880 but never published it. In 1921 Emil Post (and, independently, Ludwig Wittgenstein in the Tractatus) introduced truth tables as a decision procedure, and Post used them to prove that the propositional part of Principia is complete: every tautology is provable.

Shannon and the Switch

Claude Shannon’s master’s thesis, A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits (written 1937, published 1938), noticed that a network of relays obeys Boolean algebra. Switches in series behave like one connective and switches in parallel like another, so circuit design reduces to algebraic simplification. This may be the most consequential master’s thesis ever written: it turned logic into engineering. See Logic in Hardware.

McCulloch and Pitts

In 1943 Warren McCulloch, a neurophysiologist, and Walter Pitts, a self-taught teenage logician, published A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity. They modeled the neuron as an all-or-nothing threshold device and proved that networks of such neurons compute exactly the functions of propositional logic (and, with feedback loops, something more). Von Neumann borrowed their notation for the logic elements in his 1945 EDVAC report, and Kleene’s attempt to characterize what their looped networks can recognize gave us regular expressions. See The McCulloch-Pitts Neuron.

Cook and Satisfiability

In 1971 Stephen Cook proved (and Leonid Levin independently) that deciding whether a propositional formula can be made true, the SAT problem, is NP-complete. The humblest logic turned out to sit at the center of computational complexity theory. See SAT.

Syntax

What counts as a formula

Atoms and Connectives

The building blocks are atoms (also called propositional variables): \(p\), \(q\), \(r\), and so on. An atom stands for a statement that is either true or false, such as “the buffer is full” or “the interrupt flag is set”. The logic does not care what the statement says; it cares only how statements combine. Two constants, \(\top\) (true) and \(\bot\) (false), are often included.

Atoms combine through connectives:

Connective Symbol Read as C operator Python Rust
Negation \(\lnot p\) not \(p\) !p not p !p
Conjunction \(p \land q\) \(p\) and \(q\) p && q p and q p && q
Disjunction \(p \lor q\) \(p\) or \(q\) p || q p or q p || q
Exclusive or \(p \oplus q\) \(p\) xor \(q\) !p != !q p != q p ^ q
Conditional \(p \to q\) if \(p\) then \(q\) !p || q not p or q !p || q
Biconditional \(p \leftrightarrow q\) \(p\) if and only if \(q\) !p == !q p == q p == q

Notice that no mainstream programming language has an operator for \(\to\), even though it is arguably the most important connective in logic. Programmers express it as control flow (if) instead. C also lacks a logical XOR; the !p != !q idiom normalizes both operands to 0 or 1 first, because p != q on raw integers would treat 2 and 3 as “different truths.”

The Grammar

Here is the complete grammar in BNF. Compare it to the C grammar in Annex A of the standard.

formula  ::= atom
           | "⊤" | "⊥"
           | "¬" formula
           | "(" formula connective formula ")"
connective ::= "∧" | "∨" | "→" | "↔"
atom     ::= "p" | "q" | "r" | ...

The fully parenthesized form guarantees unique readability: every formula has exactly one parse tree, so there is never a question of what a formula means structurally. This is the property C lacks in the dangling-else problem, and the property the precedence table exists to restore.

Precedence and Associativity

Nobody writes all those parentheses. The standard convention, from tightest to loosest:

\[ \lnot \quad > \quad \land \quad > \quad \lor \quad > \quad \to \quad > \quad \leftrightarrow \]

and \(\to\) is right associative: \(p \to q \to r\) means \(p \to (q \to r)\). Right associativity is chosen because \(p \to (q \to r)\) is equivalent to \((p \land q) \to r\), so a chain of conditionals reads as “given all of these, conclude that.” (This is the same reason function types in Haskell and Rust’s Fn(A) -> Fn(B) -> C currying associate to the right.)

C agrees for the operators it has: ! binds tighter than &&, which binds tighter than ||. So !a && b || c parses as ((!a) && b) || c, exactly as \(\lnot a \land b \lor c\) would. The trouble in C is the bitwise operators, whose precedence sits below == for historical reasons; see Logical vs. Bitwise and Operator Precedence Surprises.

Parse Trees

A formula is really a tree; the string is just a serialization of it. The formula \(\lnot p \lor q \to r\) parses as \(((\lnot p) \lor q) \to r\):

The main connective is the one at the root, here \(\to\). It determines what kind of statement the formula is: this is a conditional, not a disjunction. Identifying the main connective is the first step of every proof and every truth-table evaluation, just as identifying the root of the AST is the first step of every compiler pass.

Semantics

What a formula means

Valuations

A valuation (also called an interpretation or truth assignment) is a function from atoms to \(\{T, F\}\). It is the logic’s equivalent of a program’s input. Given a valuation \(v\), the truth value of any formula is defined recursively, one clause per production in the grammar:

\[ \begin{aligned} v(\lnot \varphi) &= T \text{ iff } v(\varphi) = F \\ v(\varphi \land \psi) &= T \text{ iff } v(\varphi) = T \text{ and } v(\psi) = T \\ v(\varphi \lor \psi) &= T \text{ iff } v(\varphi) = T \text{ or } v(\psi) = T \\ v(\varphi \to \psi) &= F \text{ iff } v(\varphi) = T \text{ and } v(\psi) = F \\ v(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi) &= T \text{ iff } v(\varphi) = v(\psi) \end{aligned} \]

This is compositional semantics: the meaning of a compound is determined entirely by the meanings of its parts and the connective that joins them. It is also, line for line, a tree-walking interpreter. The semantics of propositional logic is an eval function.

An Interpreter in Python

To make that concrete, here is the whole language implemented in Python. Each grammar production becomes a class, and each semantic clause becomes an evaluate method. Python’s operator overloading lets us write p & q instead of And(p, q).

Code 
from functools import reduce
from itertools import product


class Formula:
    def __invert__(self):
        return Not(self)

    def __and__(self, other):
        return And(self, other)

    def __or__(self, other):
        return Or(self, other)

    def __rshift__(self, other):
        return Implies(self, other)

    def __xor__(self, other):
        return Xor(self, other)

    def iff(self, other):
        return Iff(self, other)

    def variables(self) -> set[str]:
        raise NotImplementedError

    def evaluate(self, valuation: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        raise NotImplementedError

    def models(self) -> list[dict[str, bool]]:
        return [valuation for valuation in Valuations(self.variables()) if self.evaluate(valuation)]

    def is_satisfiable(self) -> bool:
        return len(self.models()) > 0

    def is_tautology(self) -> bool:
        return len(self.models()) == 2 ** len(self.variables())

    def is_equivalent_to(self, other: "Formula") -> bool:
        return self.iff(other).is_tautology()


class Variable(Formula):
    def __init__(self, name: str):
        self.name = name

    def variables(self) -> set[str]:
        return {self.name}

    def evaluate(self, valuation: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        return valuation[self.name]

    def __str__(self) -> str:
        return self.name


class Not(Formula):
    def __init__(self, operand: Formula):
        self.operand = operand

    def variables(self) -> set[str]:
        return self.operand.variables()

    def evaluate(self, valuation: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        return not self.operand.evaluate(valuation)

    def __str__(self) -> str:
        return f"¬{self.operand}"


class Binary(Formula):
    symbol = "?"

    def __init__(self, left: Formula, right: Formula):
        self.left = left
        self.right = right

    def variables(self) -> set[str]:
        return self.left.variables() | self.right.variables()

    def evaluate(self, valuation: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        return self.apply(self.left.evaluate(valuation), self.right.evaluate(valuation))

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        raise NotImplementedError

    def __str__(self) -> str:
        return f"({self.left} {self.symbol} {self.right})"


class And(Binary):
    symbol = "∧"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return left and right


class Or(Binary):
    symbol = "∨"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return left or right


class Xor(Binary):
    symbol = "⊕"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return left != right


class Implies(Binary):
    symbol = "→"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return (not left) or right


class Iff(Binary):
    symbol = "↔"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return left == right


class Valuations:
    def __init__(self, variables: set[str]):
        self.variables = sorted(variables)

    def __iter__(self):
        for values in product([True, False], repeat=len(self.variables)):
            yield dict(zip(self.variables, values))


class TruthTable:
    def __init__(self, *formulas: Formula):
        self.formulas = formulas
        self.variables = sorted(set().union(*(formula.variables() for formula in formulas)))

    def headers(self) -> list[str]:
        return self.variables + [str(formula) for formula in self.formulas]

    def rows(self) -> list[list[bool]]:
        return [
            [valuation[name] for name in self.variables] + [formula.evaluate(valuation) for formula in self.formulas]
            for valuation in Valuations(set(self.variables))
        ]

    def show(self) -> None:
        headers = self.headers()
        widths = [len(header) for header in headers]
        print(" │ ".join(header.center(width) for header, width in zip(headers, widths)))
        print("─┼─".join("─" * width for width in widths))
        for row in self.rows():
            cells = ["T" if value else "F" for value in row]
            print(" │ ".join(cell.center(width) for cell, width in zip(cells, widths)))


def entails(premises: list[Formula], conclusion: Formula) -> bool:
    return (reduce(And, premises) >> conclusion).is_tautology()


p, q, r = Variable("p"), Variable("q"), Variable("r")

That is a complete implementation of the language’s syntax and semantics, plus the semantic tests (satisfiability, validity, equivalence, entailment) defined in the next few sections. Here are the basic connectives:

p │ q │ ¬p │ (p ∧ q) │ (p ∨ q) │ (p ⊕ q) │ (p → q) │ (p ↔ q)
──┼───┼────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────┼────────
T │ T │ F  │    T    │    T    │    F    │    T    │    T   
T │ F │ F  │    F    │    T    │    T    │    F    │    F   
F │ T │ T  │    F    │    T    │    T    │    T    │    F

WarningBorrowed operators keep their own precedence

Python’s >> binds tighter than &, ^, and |, which is the opposite of the logical convention for \(\to\). So p & q >> r builds \(p \land (q \to r)\), not \((p \land q) \to r\). Overloading lets you reuse an operator’s spelling, never its precedence. C++ has the same constraint, which is why every C++ DSL built on operator overloading (Boost.Spirit, expression templates, stream insertion) has a precedence wart somewhere. Parenthesize generously.

Code 
print(p & q >> r)
print((p & q) >> r)
(p ∧ (q → r))
((p ∧ q) → r)

Tautology, Contradiction, Contingency

Every formula falls into exactly one of three categories, determined by its truth table’s final column:

Category Final column Example Programming analogue
Tautology (valid) All \(T\) \(p \lor \lnot p\) An assertion that can never fire
Contradiction (unsatisfiable) All \(F\) \(p \land \lnot p\) Dead code: a branch no input reaches
Contingency Mixed \(p \to q\) An ordinary condition

A formula is satisfiable if it is not a contradiction: at least one valuation makes it true. Satisfiability and validity are duals: \(\varphi\) is a tautology exactly when \(\lnot\varphi\) is unsatisfiable. That duality is how SAT solvers prove theorems; they look for a counterexample to the negation and report success when they fail to find one.

Code 
for formula in [p | ~p, p & ~p, p >> q]:
    print(f"{str(formula):12}  tautology={formula.is_tautology()!s:5}  satisfiable={formula.is_satisfiable()}")
(p ∨ ¬p)      tautology=True   satisfiable=True
(p ∧ ¬p)      tautology=False  satisfiable=False
(p → q)       tautology=False  satisfiable=True

Logical Consequence

The central relation of logic is semantic entailment. A set of premises \(\Gamma\) entails a conclusion \(\varphi\), written \(\Gamma \models \varphi\), when every valuation that makes all the premises true also makes \(\varphi\) true. Equivalently, \(\Gamma \models \varphi\) exactly when \((\gamma_1 \land \cdots \land \gamma_n) \to \varphi\) is a tautology, which is how entails is implemented above.

Code 
print("{p → q, p} ⊨ q :", entails([p >> q, p], q))
print("{p → q, q} ⊨ p :", entails([p >> q, q], p))
{p → q, p} ⊨ q : True
{p → q, q} ⊨ p : False

The second line is the fallacy of affirming the consequent: “if it rained the street is wet; the street is wet; so it rained.” The truth table finds the counterexample (a street cleaner, \(p = F\), \(q = T\)) mechanically.

Two formulas are logically equivalent, \(\varphi \equiv \psi\), when they entail each other, which is the same as saying \(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi\) is a tautology. Equivalent formulas are interchangeable anywhere, and that is what licenses every refactoring of a boolean expression.

The Conditional

The connective everyone argues about

The material conditional \(p \to q\) is false in exactly one case: \(p\) true and \(q\) false. Two of its four rows surprise newcomers:

\(p\) \(q\) \(p \to q\)
T T T
T F F the only way to break a promise
F T T vacuously true
F F T vacuously true

Why should “if the moon is cheese, then \(2 + 2 = 4\)” be true? The best way to see that this is the right design choice is to treat \(p \to q\) as a promise or a contract. “If you pass the final, you pass the course” is broken only by a student who passes the final and still fails. A student who skipped the final has not tested the promise, so it stands. In programming terms, assert(!p || q) is the conditional, and an assertion whose guard is false does not fire.

There is a deeper reason too. We want \(\to\) to be a connective, a function of truth values alone, and we want modus ponens (from \(p\) and \(p \to q\), conclude \(q\)) to be valid. Of the 16 possible binary truth functions, the material conditional is the only one that makes modus ponens valid while also making \(p \to p\) a tautology and keeping \(p \to q\) distinct from \(q \to p\). The oddness of the false-antecedent rows is the price of being truth-functional. Logics that refuse to pay it (relevance logics, strict implication) exist, but they give up the simple semantics.

TipPython’s <= is implication

Python orders False < True, so for booleans p <= q is exactly \(p \to q\). The same holds in Rust (bool: Ord) and in C when operands are normalized to 0 and 1. It is a cute identity, not a recommended idiom: not p or q says what you mean.

Converse, Inverse, Contrapositive

A conditional has three relatives, and only one of them is equivalent to it:

Name Form Equivalent to \(p \to q\)?
Original \(p \to q\) yes
Contrapositive \(\lnot q \to \lnot p\) yes
Converse \(q \to p\) no
Inverse \(\lnot p \to \lnot q\) no (but equivalent to the converse)
Code 
for name, formula in [("contrapositive", ~q >> ~p), ("converse", q >> p), ("inverse", ~p >> ~q)]:
    print(f"{name:15} equivalent to p → q: {formula.is_equivalent_to(p >> q)}")
contrapositive  equivalent to p → q: True
converse        equivalent to p → q: False
inverse         equivalent to p → q: False
WarningNecessary and sufficient

“\(p\) only if \(q\)” means \(p \to q\), not \(q \to p\). In \(p \to q\), \(p\) is sufficient for \(q\) and \(q\) is necessary for \(p\). A guard clause if (!q) return; at the top of a function establishes that \(q\) is necessary for reaching the rest of the body: reaching the body implies \(q\). Confusing a conditional with its converse is the single most common reasoning error in code review (“this only crashes if the pointer is null; the pointer is null; so this is the crash”).

The Biconditional

\(p \leftrightarrow q\) is true when \(p\) and \(q\) agree. It is the conjunction of a conditional and its converse, \((p \to q) \land (q \to p)\), and it is the negation of XOR. Mathematicians write “iff”. In code it is == on booleans, which is why if (flag == true) is redundant: it is \(p \leftrightarrow \top\), which is equivalent to \(p\).

Laws of Equivalence

The algebra you refactor with

Because equivalent formulas are interchangeable, a list of basic equivalences turns logic into algebra: you can rewrite a formula step by step, the way you simplify a polynomial. Each law below can be checked by truth table.

Law \(\land\) form \(\lor\) form
Identity \(p \land \top \equiv p\) \(p \lor \bot \equiv p\)
Domination \(p \land \bot \equiv \bot\) \(p \lor \top \equiv \top\)
Idempotence \(p \land p \equiv p\) \(p \lor p \equiv p\)
Complement \(p \land \lnot p \equiv \bot\) \(p \lor \lnot p \equiv \top\)
Commutativity \(p \land q \equiv q \land p\) \(p \lor q \equiv q \lor p\)
Associativity \((p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)\) \((p \lor q) \lor r \equiv p \lor (q \lor r)\)
Distributivity \(p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)\) \(p \lor (q \land r) \equiv (p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)\)
Absorption \(p \land (p \lor q) \equiv p\) \(p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p\)
De Morgan \(\lnot(p \land q) \equiv \lnot p \lor \lnot q\) \(\lnot(p \lor q) \equiv \lnot p \land \lnot q\)

And the laws that eliminate the other connectives:

Law Equivalence
Double negation \(\lnot\lnot p \equiv p\)
Implication \(p \to q \equiv \lnot p \lor q\)
Contraposition \(p \to q \equiv \lnot q \to \lnot p\)
Exportation \((p \land q) \to r \equiv p \to (q \to r)\)
Biconditional \(p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \to q) \land (q \to p)\)
XOR \(p \oplus q \equiv (p \lor q) \land \lnot(p \land q)\)

The table has a striking symmetry. Swap \(\land\) with \(\lor\) and \(\top\) with \(\bot\) in any law and you get another law. This is the duality principle, and it is why the two columns mirror each other. Shannon exploited it directly; see Logic in Hardware.

The interpreter confirms all of these mechanically:

Code 
laws = {
    "De Morgan (∧)": (~(p & q), ~p | ~q),
    "De Morgan (∨)": (~(p | q), ~p & ~q),
    "Distributivity": (p & (q | r), (p & q) | (p & r)),
    "Absorption": (p | (p & q), p),
    "Exportation": ((p & q) >> r, p >> (q >> r)),
    "XOR": (p ^ q, (p | q) & ~(p & q)),
}
for name, (left, right) in laws.items():
    print(f"{name:15} {left.is_equivalent_to(right)}")

A Worked Simplification

Here is a condition of the kind that accumulates in real code as features are bolted on:

if (!(!ready || error) || (ready && !error && retry)) {
    transmit();
}

Let \(r\) = ready, \(e\) = error, \(t\) = retry. The condition is \(\lnot(\lnot r \lor e) \lor (r \land \lnot e \land t)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lnot(\lnot r \lor e) \lor (r \land \lnot e \land t) \\ &\equiv (\lnot\lnot r \land \lnot e) \lor (r \land \lnot e \land t) && \text{De Morgan} \\ &\equiv (r \land \lnot e) \lor ((r \land \lnot e) \land t) && \text{double negation, associativity} \\ &\equiv r \land \lnot e && \text{absorption} \end{aligned} \]

The retry term was dead weight all along: if (ready && !error). Absorption is the law that catches this, and it is worth knowing by sight because it is exactly the shape that redundant special cases take.

WarningLogic is commutative; C is not

The laws above hold for pure formulas. C’s && and || short-circuit: the right operand is evaluated only if the left does not already settle the answer. That makes them sequencing operators as well as logical ones, and it breaks commutativity whenever an operand has side effects or undefined behavior:

if (node != NULL && node->value > 0)   /* safe */
if (node->value > 0 && node != NULL)   /* dereferences NULL first */

Logically \(a \land b \equiv b \land a\). Operationally, the left operand of && acts as a guard for the right one. When you apply De Morgan or commute terms in real code, check that no operand depends on another having been tested first. The bitwise & and | do not short-circuit, so they are closer to pure logic, and Rust deliberately allows them on bool for exactly that reason.

Normal Forms

Two canonical shapes for every formula

A literal is an atom or its negation: \(p\), \(\lnot q\). Every formula is equivalent to one in each of two standard shapes:

  • Disjunctive normal form (DNF): an OR of ANDs of literals, such as \((p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q)\). Each AND term describes one way to make the formula true.
  • Conjunctive normal form (CNF): an AND of ORs of literals, such as \((p \lor q) \land (\lnot p \lor \lnot q)\). Each OR term, called a clause, is a constraint that must hold.

Both examples are \(p \oplus q\).

Reading DNF Off a Truth Table

DNF is easy to construct: take each row where the formula is true, write the conjunction of literals that describes that row, and OR them together. For XOR the true rows are \((T, F)\) and \((F, T)\), giving \((p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q)\). This is the sum of products form that digital designers use, and its existence is a constructive proof that \(\{\lnot, \land, \lor\}\) can express every truth function.

CNF comes from the false rows by duality: each false row contributes a clause that rules it out.

Converting by Rewriting

For an arbitrary formula the textbook algorithm has three steps:

  1. Eliminate \(\leftrightarrow\) and \(\to\) using the biconditional and implication laws.
  2. Push negations inward with De Morgan and double negation until \(\lnot\) applies only to atoms. This is negation normal form.
  3. Distribute \(\lor\) over \(\land\) (for CNF) or \(\land\) over \(\lor\) (for DNF).

Example: convert \((p \to q) \to r\) to CNF.

\[ \begin{aligned} (p \to q) \to r &\equiv \lnot(\lnot p \lor q) \lor r && \text{implication, twice} \\ &\equiv (p \land \lnot q) \lor r && \text{De Morgan, double negation} \\ &\equiv (p \lor r) \land (\lnot q \lor r) && \text{distribution} \end{aligned} \]

Why Solvers Want CNF

CNF matters because it is the input format of SAT solvers. A clause is a constraint, and a CNF formula is a list of constraints that must all hold, which is a natural way to encode real problems (“at least one of these packages must be installed”, “these two cannot both be”). The standard DIMACS file format is nothing but a list of clauses with atoms numbered \(1, 2, 3, \ldots\) and negation written as a minus sign.

The catch is step 3: distribution can blow up exponentially. \((p_1 \land q_1) \lor (p_2 \land q_2) \lor \cdots \lor (p_n \land q_n)\) has \(2^n\) clauses in CNF. The fix, due to Grigori Tseitin (1968), is to introduce a fresh atom for each subformula. The result is not equivalent to the original (it has extra atoms), but it is equisatisfiable (satisfiable exactly when the original is) and only linearly larger. Every practical solver front end does this.

Functional Completeness

How few connectives do you need?

With two inputs there are \(2^{2^2} = 16\) possible truth functions. Here they all are, with outputs listed for \((p, q) = (T,T), (T,F), (F,T), (F,F)\):

A set of connectives is functionally complete if every truth function, of any number of inputs, can be built from it. The DNF construction shows \(\{\lnot, \land, \lor\}\) is complete. De Morgan lets you drop one of \(\land\) and \(\lor\), so \(\{\lnot, \land\}\) and \(\{\lnot, \lor\}\) are complete too.

The surprise is that a single connective suffices. NAND alone works, because

\[ \lnot p \equiv p \uparrow p \qquad\qquad p \land q \equiv (p \uparrow q) \uparrow (p \uparrow q) \]

where \(\uparrow\) is NAND. Since \(\{\lnot, \land\}\) is complete, so is \(\{\uparrow\}\). NOR works by the dual argument. They are the only two binary connectives with this property.

class Nand(Binary):
    symbol = "↑"

    def apply(self, left: bool, right: bool) -> bool:
        return not (left and right)


def nand_not(operand: Formula) -> Formula:
    return Nand(operand, operand)


def nand_and(left: Formula, right: Formula) -> Formula:
    return nand_not(Nand(left, right))


def nand_or(left: Formula, right: Formula) -> Formula:
    return Nand(nand_not(left), nand_not(right))

Post’s Criterion

Which sets are not complete, and why? Emil Post answered this completely in 1941. There are five properties that are preserved under composition, so a set of connectives that all share one of them can never escape it:

Property Meaning Example of a trapped set
Preserves \(F\) all-\(F\) inputs give \(F\) \(\{\land, \lor, \oplus\}\)
Preserves \(T\) all-\(T\) inputs give \(T\) \(\{\land, \lor, \to\}\)
Monotone flipping an input from \(F\) to \(T\) never flips the output from \(T\) to \(F\) \(\{\land, \lor\}\)
Self-dual negating all inputs negates the output \(\{\lnot\}\)
Affine expressible with only \(\oplus\) and constants \(\{\oplus, \lnot\}\)

A set is complete if and only if it escapes all five: for each property, it contains at least one connective that lacks it. NAND lacks all five by itself, which is why it is complete alone. \(\{\land, \lor\}\) is monotone, so no amount of AND and OR can ever produce NOT; that is a precise version of the intuition that you need inversion somewhere. \(\{\to\}\) preserves \(T\), so it cannot make \(\bot\), but \(\{\to, \bot\}\) is complete.

Inference and Proof

Deriving truths without truth tables

Truth tables decide entailment, but they cost \(2^n\) rows for \(n\) atoms. Thirty atoms is a billion rows. Proof is the alternative: start from premises and apply rules that are known to preserve truth, one step at a time, until the conclusion appears. Proofs are syntactic; they manipulate symbols without ever consulting a valuation.

Rules of Inference

Rule Premises Conclusion
Modus ponens \(p \to q\), \(p\) \(q\)
Modus tollens \(p \to q\), \(\lnot q\) \(\lnot p\)
Hypothetical syllogism \(p \to q\), \(q \to r\) \(p \to r\)
Disjunctive syllogism \(p \lor q\), \(\lnot p\) \(q\)
Conjunction \(p\), \(q\) \(p \land q\)
Simplification \(p \land q\) \(p\)
Addition \(p\) \(p \lor q\)
Resolution \(p \lor q\), \(\lnot p \lor r\) \(q \lor r\)

Every rule in the table is itself an entailment that a truth table would confirm. Their value is that they compose: a ten-step proof can be checked by eye even when its truth table would have thousands of rows.

A Worked Proof

Premises: \(p \to q\), \(q \to r\), \(\lnot r\). Prove \(\lnot p\).

Step Formula Justification
1 \(p \to q\) premise
2 \(q \to r\) premise
3 \(\lnot r\) premise
4 \(p \to r\) hypothetical syllogism, 1, 2
5 \(\lnot p\) modus tollens, 4, 3

In code-review terms: “if the config loads, the driver initializes; if the driver initializes, the LED blinks; the LED is not blinking; therefore the config did not load.” Debugging is mostly modus tollens.

Resolution

Resolution deserves special mention because it is the one rule a machine needs. Given two clauses that contain an atom and its negation, it produces a clause with that atom removed. To prove \(\Gamma \models \varphi\), convert \(\Gamma \cup \{\lnot\varphi\}\) to CNF and resolve repeatedly. If you ever derive the empty clause (a contradiction), the entailment holds. This procedure is refutation complete: for propositional logic it always terminates with the right answer. It is the basis of Prolog’s execution model and, in a much refined form, of modern SAT solvers.

Soundness and Completeness

There are now two notions of “follows from”:

  • \(\Gamma \models \varphi\): semantic entailment, defined by truth.
  • \(\Gamma \vdash \varphi\): derivability, defined by proof.

A proof system is sound if \(\Gamma \vdash \varphi\) implies \(\Gamma \models \varphi\) (it proves nothing false) and complete if \(\Gamma \models \varphi\) implies \(\Gamma \vdash \varphi\) (it can prove everything true). Standard proof systems for propositional logic are both, as Post showed in 1921. That match between syntax and semantics is the property every type system aspires to and few achieve: Rust’s borrow checker, for instance, is sound but deliberately incomplete, rejecting some programs that are actually safe.

Propositional logic is also decidable: an algorithm (the truth table) always answers whether \(\Gamma \models \varphi\). First-order logic keeps soundness and completeness but loses decidability, which is one of the deepest results of the 1930s and the reason the jump to predicate logic is a jump in kind, not just in expressiveness.

Logic in Code

Where the formulas hide in programs

Every condition in a program is a propositional formula whose atoms are comparisons. The language-level differences are in how strictly a language separates truth values from everything else.

Two entries deserve comment.

Python’s and and or return operands. x or default evaluates to x if x is truthy, else default. That is a useful idiom, but it means and/or are not boolean connectives; they are short-circuiting selection operators that happen to agree with logic when the operands are bool. 0 or None is None, not False.

C++ loses short-circuiting when && is overloaded. A user-defined operator&& is an ordinary function call, so both operands are evaluated. This is why Sutter and Alexandrescu’s C++ Coding Standards (Item 30) says never to overload &&, ||, or ,.

De Morgan in Practice

De Morgan’s laws are the refactoring you will use most. Loop conditions are the classic case, because the condition to continue is the negation of the condition to stop:

/* stop when the buffer is full or we see the terminator */
while (!(count == capacity || byte == '\0')) { ... }

/* De Morgan: continue while not full and not terminator */
while (count != capacity && byte != '\0') { ... }

The second form is easier to read because each negation has been pushed onto an atom, where it disappears into the comparison operator. This is negation normal form applied to source code.

Guard Clauses as Conditionals

An early return encodes an implication about the rest of the function:

fn transmit(frame: &Frame, link: &Link) -> Result<(), Error> {
    if !link.is_up() {
        return Err(Error::LinkDown);
    }
    if frame.len() > link.mtu() {
        return Err(Error::TooLarge);
    }
    link.send(frame)
}

Reaching link.send implies \(\textit{up} \land \textit{fits}\). Every guard you pass adds a conjunct to what you know, which is the same reasoning a compiler’s flow analysis does when it narrows a type after a check (TypeScript’s narrowing, Rust’s if let).

Logic in Hardware

Shannon’s insight

Shannon’s thesis observed that a relay contact is a propositional atom: it is either closed or open. Two contacts in series conduct only if both are closed, and two in parallel conduct if either is. Series is \(\land\), parallel is \(\lor\), and a normally closed contact is \(\lnot\). A circuit is therefore a formula, and the laws of equivalence become a way to remove relays.

Shannon actually used the dual algebra. He measured a circuit’s hindrance, with 0 for a closed (conducting) path and 1 for an open one, so series became addition and parallel became multiplication. Because of the duality principle the choice does not matter, and the algebra is identical up to swapping \(\land\) with \(\lor\) and \(\top\) with \(\bot\). Engineers later settled on the conducting-is-1 convention that we use today.

Transistors replaced relays, but the mapping did not change. In CMOS, the cheapest gates to build are NAND and NOR (four transistors each), while AND and OR are a NAND or NOR followed by an inverter. So hardware designers think in NAND, and the functional completeness of NAND is not a curiosity: it is why a chip can be built from a sea of identical cells.

A Half Adder

Adding two one-bit numbers \(a\) and \(b\) produces a sum bit and a carry bit:

\(a\) \(b\) carry sum
0 0 0 0
0 1 0 1
1 0 0 1
1 1 1 0

Reading off the columns, \(\textit{sum} = a \oplus b\) and \(\textit{carry} = a \land b\). Arithmetic, at the bottom, is propositional logic. Chain these into full adders and you have the ALU, which is how Boole’s “algebra of thought” ended up running every + you have ever compiled.

The multiplexer in the glossary is another formula in disguise: a 2-to-1 mux with select \(s\) computes \((\lnot s \land a) \lor (s \land b)\), which is the hardware form of the ternary operator s ? b : a.

The McCulloch-Pitts Neuron

Logic as a model of the brain

McCulloch and Pitts made five idealizations about neurons, the central ones being that a neuron is all-or-none (it fires or it does not), that it fires when the number of active excitatory inputs reaches a fixed threshold, and that any active inhibitory input absolutely prevents firing. Time advances in discrete steps, and a neuron’s output at step \(t+1\) depends on its inputs at step \(t\).

Under those assumptions a neuron is a boolean function, and the right threshold turns it into a logic gate:

Gate Excitatory inputs Inhibitory inputs Threshold
AND \(a, b\) none 2
OR \(a, b\) none 1
NOT none \(a\) 0
\(a \land \lnot b\) \(a\) \(b\) 1

The NOT unit is worth a second look: with threshold 0 it fires spontaneously, and the only thing that stops it is the inhibitory input. Here is the 1943 model in code:

Code 
class ThresholdUnit:
    def __init__(self, excitatory: list[str], inhibitory: list[str], threshold: int):
        self.excitatory = excitatory
        self.inhibitory = inhibitory
        self.threshold = threshold

    def fires(self, inputs: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        if any(inputs[name] for name in self.inhibitory):
            return False
        return sum(inputs[name] for name in self.excitatory) >= self.threshold


class NeuronTable:
    def __init__(self, units: dict[str, ThresholdUnit], input_names: list[str]):

            inputs = dict(zip(self.input_names, values))
            outputs = [int(unit.fires(inputs)) for unit in self.units.values()]
            cells = [str(int(value)) for value in values] + [str(output).center(len(name)) for name, output in zip(self.units, outputs)]
            print("  ".join(cells))

Since AND, OR, and NOT are functionally complete, any propositional formula can be computed by a network of these units. That was the paper’s first main result. The second, that networks with loops (“circles”, in their words) can hold state and so behave like finite machines, is the one that led Kleene to regular expressions. The grep patterns in the shell chapter are, by direct descent, a notation for what looped McCulloch-Pitts networks can recognize.

Why XOR Needs a Network

No single unit computes XOR. The argument is short. If either input is inhibitory, then turning on that input alone silences the unit, but XOR must fire on a single active input. So both inputs are excitatory, and the unit fires when the count reaches the threshold. XOR must fire on a count of 1 and stay silent on a count of 2, and a threshold cannot do that: anything reaching 1 also reaches 2. A brute-force search over every configuration confirms it:

Code 
def xor(inputs: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
    return inputs["a"] != inputs["b"]


def all_input_combinations() -> list[dict[str, bool]]:
    return [dict(zip(["a", "b"], values)) for values in product([False, True], repeat=2)]


def computes(unit: ThresholdUnit, target) -> bool:
    return all(unit.fires(inputs) == target(inputs) for inputs in all_input_combinations())


roles = ["excitatory", "inhibitory", "unused"]
candidates = [
    ThresholdUnit(

Two layers solve it, using the equivalence \(p \oplus q \equiv (p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q)\):

Code 
class TwoLayerNetwork:
    def __init__(self, hidden: dict[str, ThresholdUnit], output: ThresholdUnit):
        self.hidden = hidden
        self.output = output

    def fires(self, inputs: dict[str, bool]) -> bool:
        hidden_outputs = {name: unit.fires(inputs) for name, unit in self.hidden.items()}
        return self.output.fires(hidden_outputs)


xor_network = TwoLayerNetwork(
    hidden={"a_only": ThresholdUnit(["a"], ["b"], 1), "b_only": ThresholdUnit(["b"], ["a"], 1)},

)

This tiny fact has a large history. Rosenblatt’s perceptron (1958) replaced the fixed thresholds with learnable real-valued weights. In Perceptrons (1969), Minsky and Papert proved that a single-layer perceptron can only compute linearly separable functions, and XOR is the canonical function that is not. The book is widely blamed (somewhat unfairly, since it said nothing against multilayer networks) for a decade-long collapse in neural network funding. The fix, multilayer networks trained by backpropagation, became practical in the 1980s, and the modern deep learning stack descends from it. The McCulloch-Pitts unit with real weights and a smooth activation function in place of a hard threshold is, structurally, the neuron in every network today.

NoteWhat the model got right and wrong

As neuroscience, McCulloch and Pitts were wrong in most details: real neurons are not binary, inhibition is not absolute, and timing is continuous. As computer science they were right in the way that mattered: they showed that a network of simple threshold devices is a universal propositional computer. Von Neumann took that idea, swapped neurons for vacuum tubes, and used their notation in the design of the EDVAC.

SAT

The problem at the center of complexity

The Boolean satisfiability problem asks: given a formula, is there a valuation that makes it true? The truth table answers it in \(O(2^n)\) time. Checking a proposed answer, by contrast, takes linear time: plug in the values and evaluate. Problems with that shape (hard to solve, easy to verify) form the class NP, and Cook’s theorem says SAT is the hardest of them: every NP problem can be translated into SAT in polynomial time. Nobody knows a polynomial algorithm for SAT, and finding one (or proving none exists) is the P versus NP problem.

In theory, then, SAT is hopeless. In practice it is one of the great success stories of computer science. Modern CDCL solvers (conflict-driven clause learning, from GRASP in 1996 and Chaff in 2001) routinely solve industrial instances with millions of variables. Hardness is a worst-case statement, and the formulas that come from real problems have structure that solvers exploit.

DPLL

Every modern solver descends from the Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland procedure (1962). It is a backtracking search over CNF with two inferences that prune the tree:

  • Unit propagation: if a clause has only one unassigned literal left and the rest are false, that literal must be true. Assign it and simplify. This is modus ponens in clause form, and it does most of the work.
  • Branching: when propagation stalls, pick an unassigned atom, try it true, and backtrack to false if that leads to an empty clause.

Clauses use the DIMACS convention: atoms are positive integers and \(\lnot x\) is \(-x\).

Code 
Clause = frozenset[int]


class DpllSolver:
    def __init__(self, clauses: list[Clause]):
        self.clauses = clauses

    def solve(self) -> dict[int, bool] | None:
        return self.search(self.clauses, {})

    def search(self, clauses: list[Clause], assignment: dict[int, bool]) -> dict[int, bool] | None:
        clauses, assignment = self.propagate_units(clauses, assignment)
        if clauses is None:
            return None
        if not clauses:
            return assignment
        literal = next(iter(clauses[0]))
        for choice in (literal, -literal):
            result = self.search(self.assign(clauses, choice), {**assignment, abs(choice): choice > 0})
            if result is not None:
                return result
        return None

    def propagate_units(self, clauses, assignment):
        while clauses is not None:
            unit = next((clause for clause in clauses if len(clause) == 1), None)
            if unit is None:
                return clauses, assignment
            literal = next(iter(unit))
            clauses = self.assign(clauses, literal)
            assignment = {**assignment, abs(literal): literal > 0}
        return None, assignment

    @staticmethod
    def assign(clauses: list[Clause] | None, literal: int) -> list[Clause] | None:
        if clauses is None:
            return None
        simplified = []
        for clause in clauses:
            if literal in clause:
                continue
            reduced = clause - {-literal}
            if not reduced:
                return None
            simplified.append(reduced)
        return simplified

An Example: Dependency Resolution

SAT solvers are hiding inside tools you use. openSUSE’s libsolv (behind zypper and Fedora’s dnf), conda, and Eclipse’s plugin manager all resolve package dependencies by translating them into CNF. Here is a toy version. An app needs a logging library, which comes in versions 1 and 2. Version 2 needs a newer runtime; version 1 conflicts with a TLS library the app also needs.

Code 
class PackageProblem:
    def __init__(self, packages: list[str]):
        self.number_of = {name: index + 1 for index, name in enumerate(packages)}
        self.name_of = {number: name for name, number in self.number_of.items()}
        self.clauses: list[Clause] = []

    def require(self, package: str) -> None:
        self.clauses.append(frozenset({self.number_of[package]}))

    def depends(self, package: str, *alternatives: str) -> None:
        self.clauses.append(frozenset({-self.number_of[package]} | {self.number_of[name] for name in alternatives}))

    def conflicts(self, first: str, second: str) -> None:
        self.clauses.append(frozenset({-self.number_of[first], -self.number_of[second]}))

    def resolve(self) -> list[str] | None:
        assignment = DpllSolver(self.clauses).solve()
        if assignment is None:
            return None
        return sorted(self.name_of[number] for number, installed in assignment.items() if installed)


problem = PackageProblem(["app", "log-v1", "log-v2", "tls", "runtime-new"])
problem.require("app")
problem.depends("app", "log-v1", "log-v2")
problem.depends("app", "tls")
problem.conflicts("log-v1", "log-v2")
problem.conflicts("log-v1", "tls")
problem.depends("log-v2", "runtime-new")
print("install:", problem.resolve())

problem.conflicts("runtime-new", "tls")
print("after adding a runtime/tls conflict:", problem.resolve())
install: ['app', 'log-v2', 'runtime-new', 'tls']
after adding a runtime/tls conflict: None

Each constraint is one clause: “app depends on log v1 or v2” is \(\lnot\textit{app} \lor \textit{log}_1 \lor \textit{log}_2\), which is the implication law applied to \(\textit{app} \to (\textit{log}_1 \lor \textit{log}_2)\), and “these conflict” is \(\lnot a \lor \lnot b\). When the last conflict makes the problem unsatisfiable, the solver proves that no installation exists, which a hand-written greedy resolver cannot do. Real package resolution adds preferences (newest version first), which turns it into an optimization problem, but the core is exactly this.

Other places SAT and its descendants (SMT solvers such as Z3, which add arithmetic and data types on top of SAT) do real work: hardware verification (checking that an optimized circuit is equivalent to its specification), bounded model checking of C programs, scheduling, cryptanalysis, and the occasional mathematical proof. In 2016 a SAT solver settled the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem with a 200-terabyte proof.

What Propositional Logic Cannot Say

Propositional logic treats “the buffer is full” as an indivisible atom. It cannot look inside. That makes some obviously valid arguments impossible to express:

Every interrupt handler must be short. uart_isr is an interrupt handler. Therefore uart_isr must be short.

Rendered propositionally, the three sentences are three unrelated atoms \(p\), \(q\), \(r\), and \(\{p, q\} \not\models r\). The argument’s validity lives in the words “every” and in the shared name uart_isr, which propositional logic has no way to represent.

Predicate logic (first-order logic) fixes this by adding objects, predicates over objects, and the quantifiers \(\forall\) (“for all”) and \(\exists\) (“there exists”):

\[ \forall x\, (\textit{Handler}(x) \to \textit{Short}(x)), \; \textit{Handler}(\texttt{uart\_isr}) \models \textit{Short}(\texttt{uart\_isr}) \]

The cost is steep. First-order logic is still sound and complete (Gödel, 1929), but it is no longer decidable (Church and Turing, 1936): there is no truth table to fall back on, and no algorithm that always terminates with the answer. The jump from propositional to predicate logic is the jump from a language where every question can be settled to one where some cannot, and it is the same jump, by the same people, that defined what a computer is. See The Theoretical Foundations.

  • Herbert Enderton, A Mathematical Introduction to Logic is the rigorous treatment: unique readability, compactness, and completeness proved rather than asserted. Read it when the “why” behind soundness and completeness starts to itch.

Hardware and History

  • Charles Petzold, Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software builds a computer from relays upward, retracing Shannon’s insight in a way that makes it feel inevitable.
  • The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on Ancient Logic (for the Stoics and Philo’s conditional) and Classical Logic are authoritative and free.

SAT in Practice

  • Donald Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4B devotes a long section to satisfiability, with algorithms, encodings, and history in Knuth’s usual depth.
  • PySAT gives Python access to industrial solvers such as CaDiCaL and Glucose. Reimplementing the package example above with it is a good exercise in CNF encoding.
  • Z3 (Microsoft Research) is the SMT solver to try next; its Python bindings let you state constraints over integers and bit vectors as well as booleans.

Practice

  • Take a gnarly condition from code you maintain, translate it into a formula, simplify it with the laws, and check your result with the TruthTable class above.
  • Extend the interpreter with a to_cnf method that implements the three-step conversion, then feed its output to DpllSolver.
  • Encode a Sudoku as CNF (729 atoms, one per cell-digit pair) and solve it. It is the classic first SAT encoding, and it shows how much structure unit propagation exploits.
